Building signals and images from waves

CS 5330, Week 8. Two small experiments with the discrete Fourier transform (DFT): rebuild a 1D signal from a handful of sinusoids, then take apart an 8 × 8 greyscale image one frequency at a time.

Part 1: Rebuilding a 1D signal from sinusoids

The black stems are a signal with N = 32 samples. Your job is to rebuild it by adding cosines. Each harmonic k completes exactly k cycles across the 32 samples, and you control its amplitude and phase. The red curve is your sum.

x̂[n] = A0 + Σk Ak cos(2πkn/N + φk)

Things to try

  1. Start with Two tones and rebuild it using only the sliders. The spectrum tells you which harmonics matter. How close can you get to zero error before you look at the phases?
  2. Switch to Square wave and press Fill from the DFT. Why are all the even harmonics (apart from k = 0) exactly zero?
  3. In the second plot, step K up slowly for the square wave. Watch the overshoot next to each jump. Adding more terms makes it narrower, but it does not go away while you are still short of K = 16.
  4. At K = 16 the error is zero, yet the red curve still wiggles between samples. Why is that consistent with a perfect reconstruction?
  5. Compare Short pulse with Triangle. Which one needs more harmonics to look right, and what does that tell you about sharp changes and high frequencies?

Part 2: The 2D DFT of an 8 × 8 image

An 8 × 8 greyscale image has 64 pixels, and its 2D DFT has 64 coefficients F(u, v). Each coefficient weights one 2D wave: u counts cycles across the image horizontally and v counts cycles vertically. Draw on the image or load a preset, then click any coefficient to see the wave it stands for.

F(u, v) = Σx Σy f(x, y) e−j2π(ux + vy)/8

For a real image, F(−u, −v) is the complex conjugate of F(u, v). The two always travel together, so including or excluding one does the same to its mirror partner. That keeps the reconstruction real.

Things to try

  1. Load Vertical stripes. The stripes change along x, so where in the magnitude plot does the energy end up? Now load Horizontal stripes and predict the answer before you look.
  2. Load Checkerboard. Why does all the energy (apart from the average) sit in a corner? What is the highest frequency an 8 × 8 grid can represent?
  3. Load Single pixel. The magnitude is flat, so where is the information about the pixel's position stored? Click a few coefficients and compare their phases.
  4. Load Centred square and keep 9 coefficients, first with "lowest frequencies first", then with "largest magnitudes first". Which looks better, and which has lower error? JPEG compresses 8 × 8 blocks with a close cousin of this transform (the DCT).
  5. Keeping only low frequencies is a low-pass filter. Describe what happens to the edges of the square. How does this connect to the Gaussian blur from today's reading?
